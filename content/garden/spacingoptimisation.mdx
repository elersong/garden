## Why does timing matter for retention at all?

Short answer. Yes.

Hermann Ebbinghaus demonstrated in 1880 that memory decays over time without 
reinforcement and follows a predictable pattern. A 2015 replication confirmed 
that the shape holds across subjects in modern day. Those findings imply that if memory
deterioration follows a curve, then reviewing material before it fully decays should
interrupt that curve and reset it.

> Murre, J. M. J., & Dros, J. (2015). Replication and analysis of Ebbinghaus' 
> forgetting curve. *PLOS ONE*, *10*(7), e0120644. 
> https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644

## How much does the gap between review sessions matter?

It matters enough to be optimizable! A paper from 2008 found that the optimal gap between 
study sessions isn't a fixed value; it scales with how long you need to retain the 
material. For a one-week retention window, the optimal gap is roughly 20–40% of that 
window. For a one-year window, it drops to about 5–10%. The gap that feels 
productively challenging in the short term is not the same gap that produces durable 
long term retention. Research seems to support expanding intervals over time for the most 
long term payoff.

> Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing 
> effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological 
> Science*, *19*(11), 1095–1102. 
> https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x

## Is spaced repetition the right tool for building associative networks?

Spaced repetition optimizes for *when* to review, but it doesn't say anything about 
*what to review together*. A real-world study on context-dependent memory found that 
low-frequency encounters benefit most from specific, repetitive context. So the less often you 
encounter something, the more the surrounding context shapes how you retrieve it later.

## Does intuition about spacing hold up against the research?

Mostly.

Research confirms that longer gaps produce more durable retention than shorter ones, **up to a point.** 
What the Cepeda (2008) research added was the deceleration: the ratio of optimal gap to retention 
window actually shrinks as the retention window grows. 

That nuance changes the Leitner box schedule from a clean doubling pattern (1-2-4-8-16 days) to a 
deliberately tapered one (1-3-7-14-30), where early gaps are aggressive and later gaps grow more
 slowly than a simple multiplier would suggest. So further review is probably necessary to narrow in
 on the best review schedule for long term retention.
