## What sent me down this rabbithole?

I keep running into the tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon, and I fear it's getting worse with age. I conceptually know something relevant is in there but I'm finding it increasingly harder to pull to consciousness. The more I learn about how brains and memory are architected, the more I think it's less of a storage problem (although I'm nervous of that too) and more of an access problem. 

I tend to find that I can describe the concept to someone, down to the letter of the alphabet the word starts with. When I can get them to say it, I immediately know that's the idea which was on the other side of thick mental door. Clinically, this is characterized as "diminished recall" (Spielman et al, 2017). Personally, it's characterized as a real pain in the... head.

My working hypothesis is that I don't re-touch concepts often enough to keep the paths to them clear. 

## What does a memory "network" even look like?

When I play music by ear, I don't always have to think about which key on the piano to press next. Though my chops are pretty rusty lately, as I focus on my career, I can still play some things entirely from muscle memory. And I can figure new songs out almost entirely from muscle memory. The associations between sounds and shapes of my fingers mean I can play in a key without having to figure out all the sharps and flats beforehand. (Heh. before hand)

Thorough enough co-exposure to stimulus means that tugging on one idea pulls on the idea glued to it. 

When I bake cookies, I hear my mom telling me that salt makes sweet things taste sweeter, so never forget it. When I'm stuck on a math puzzle, I hear my dad telling me that a complicated problem is nothing more than a series of smaller, easier problems. And if I select the smaller problems precisely, they will almost solve themselves. And then I roll my eyes, knowing that rule is always correct.

People who know anything about neurons often repeat the axiom that "neurons that fire together wire together." Advertising works because regular exposure to a stimulus makes it easier to accept, and that makes things easier to remember. So if the principle holds, then maybe controlling what goes into my brain and when could give me the power to shape what I remember... and how I remember it.

## Is scheduled review a real response to this problem?

I haven't had time to dig really far into the research yet. But given the personal and third-party anecdotal evidence of using flash cards to study for a wide array of topics, I fell pretty confident that review is a great tool for putting ideas in a brain. 

Given the work of Ebbinghaus, I know that staggered-interval spaced-repetition methodologies allow for better outcomes with less time spent cycling. Now, the only question is whether or not grouping ideas together for review can tie them together for easier recall. Maybe I can't remember the idea outright, but if I have two or three other conceptual pathways to get there, I can shake it loose.

I don't actually know with any confidence yet, but at this point, something like a flashcard app could be a really simple, inexpensive way to combat my tip of tongue problem long term. I could potentially stop losing words and maybe even remember more math and science topics!
