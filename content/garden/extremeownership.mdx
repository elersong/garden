## before diving too deep into the framework, what are it's core tenants?

1. When something goes wrong below a leader, it's the leaders fault. 
2. A leader's job is to make sure suboordinates understand how what they're being asked to do connects to the collective goal.
3. If a leader doesn't believe in the mission, they can't expect anyone else to.
4. If a leader's ego is getting in the way of the right call, they must sacrifice the ego.
5. Sometimes someone just isn't going to fit. The leader to make that call, even when it's uncomfortable. (This one feels icky, bc potential abuse.)
6. What a leader tolerates becomes standard, setting the constraints of what's acceptable.
7. If a leader has a problem with an order coming from above, their job is to figure out why it makes sense before deciding it doesn't.
8. A leader is responsible for managing not only resources, but the relationships those resources carry within the context of the mission. (Ch 5)


## Is the Extreme Ownership framework a "useful fiction" for decision-making, or is it meant to be a universal truth about causality? Does that even really matter?

In Extreme Ownership Ch 2, Babin really seems sincere in his belief that the framework has no downsides.

> Babin & Willink (2015)

## "Leaders take the blame. Suboordinates take the credit." Is that sustainable?

I can see the benefit in this take from Extreme Ownership Ch 1, but it feels like a position that suffers from a foundational
attribution error. That kind of bias can result in long-term weaknesses, like a martyrship complex. Does that still result in benefits
when the situation isn't life or death?

> Spielman, R., Dumper, K., Jenkins, W., Lacombe, A., Lovett, M. & Perlmutter, M. (2017). _Psychology_. OpenStax College, Rice University.

## How much of the framework is built upon survivorship bias? 

The authors present compelling anecdotes that support the efficacy claims they make, but I'm not immediately sold on the universality of 
the framework outside of business or war. Am I able to learn of this leadership tool because it's universally applicable? Or merely because
the authors happened to make it home? 

So far, I see a lot of correlation, and compelling correlation at that, but not officially causation. 

## When does the framework apply, and when does it not?

Teams that are already failing seem like a natural fit for any such intervention. Combat teams feel like they could innately benefit from absolute
thinking as patterns to rely on in moments where immediate action saves lives. But how does this way of thinking apply to teams that are already
healthy? Solo work?

## Is the Extreme Ownership framework the actual CAUSE of better outcomes, or does it happen to be one method of creating belief, clarity and high standards which would also provide the expected results? 

Actually, the authors never (at least so far) claim that their framework is the only way to provide winning results. They just present that for them
this framework has consistently produced results in circumstances when winning truly mattered. 

## ego is framed in many ways as an adversary, but can confidence, conviction, making unpopular decisions, etc be a useful type of ego? Where's the line?

The line feels reasonably fuzzy, but maybe that's ok. Maybe the most useful heuristic isn't "where is the exact line?" but instead a more general
"which direction am I moving in?". Maybe an ego is healthy if it **grows capability and conviction**. Conversely, maybe an unhealthy ego is one that
**defends my opinions at the cost of the progress/outcome** toward the goal. 

> Chapter 4

## Who get to decide what a good fit is? And how can that remain objective?

The authors discuss the deleterious effect dissenting voices can have on morale and effectiveness of the group. In many ways that makes clear sense.
An underperforming team member can become a punishment for those contributing well to make up for, or a reason for them to coast. Without approaching
with an unchecked ego, this feels like it can become a mechanism to eliminate challenge. 

The authors never dive deeply into a core heuristic, opting to rely on individual resolve without any standard of comparison. So, a person who applies
their power as a leader to bolster their ego rather than the effectiveness of the team simply can't meet the requirements of "a good leader." I can't say 
that I disagree with an opinion such as that, but it would have liked to read what the authors use as their internal anchor points.
