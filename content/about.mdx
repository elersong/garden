I'm an experienced engineer / CS student in Utah, re-approaching a bachelor's degree while building
toward economic independence through software. I found software the same way I find anything: **raw curiosity.**
I've discovered most of my life experiences worth finding that way, but the instinct
has a cost. Simple things tend to bore me once I've figured them out. That probably
explains why I opted for a digital garden instead of a portfolio. Everybody has a portfolio,
but I think they all choose to display the destination when **the journey** is the more interesting part.

When I build software things, I obsess over the person hidden on the other side of the screen and
the many shapes they can take. The user with all their faculties and some background understanding is
easy to build for. **The far edges** of the user base really tell me if my ideas work, and as 
a proponent of the [curb-cut effect](https://www.levelaccess.com/uncategorized/the-curb-cut-effect-how-digital-accessibility-elevates-ux-for-everyone/), 
I find that designing for them makes every product better. Not 
every accessibility convention is a joy to implement, but the people who need them got no 
say in the matter. The cyberscape is for all of us, and I get to open the door.

Some people have found a problem to solve that doesn't feel like work to them; **I haven't found 
mine yet.** That keeps me moving. There's a purpose or two for me out there, and I'll either find them
or I'll build them myself. Programming suits me, because there's always a harder challenge just
beyond my last victory. It's given me years of slow, consistent upgrades to the way I think: about math,
about people, about thinking. There's enough left to learn that I could spend my life on it. I think 
I will. 

I'm going to end up somewhere the problems are hard and the people uplift each other to meet them. I'm 
meant to work with those who build well, because that's who they are. I'll be ready if I find it, and 
**I'll just build it myself** if I don't.
